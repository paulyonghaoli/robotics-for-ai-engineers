## A. Why this lab exists

[Lesson 0.1](01-what-changes.md) lists four assumptions that break when a model starts driving something. Reading them is easy. Recognising one at 11pm, from a symptom, when three plausible explanations are on the table and one of them is "buy a better sensor" — that is the skill.

So this lab gives you three systems that fail on a robot, each with an offline story that looks entirely convincing. Every one is the *code* being wrong about the world, not the model being weak. In all three cases the obvious remedy — more data, a better camera, a re-tuned gain — makes no difference.

## B. The diagnostic table

| Symptom | The tempting explanation | The actual assumption that broke |
|---|---|---|
| Great held-out metric, fails immediately on the robot | overfitting; need more data | **your outputs change your inputs** — it was scored on someone else's states |
| Fails at speed, fine when slowed down | perception isn't accurate enough at speed | **latency is correctness** — the measurement describes the past |
| Selection script picked a controller that broke something | bad luck; tighten the threshold | **mistakes are physical** — a constraint was averaged into an objective |
| Estimate looks perfect in replay, drifts live | model mismatch | **the state is hidden** — replay had ground truth available |

97% action agreement on a genuinely held-out set, and the car ends up 49 m outside a 1 m lane. Nothing is overfitted. The test set contains the states the *expert* drove through, and the policy will never see those once it is the one steering — its own small errors take it somewhere else, and the next state comes from there. The gust in this world is one the expert recovers from without difficulty; the policy cannot, because recovery states never appear in data collected by someone who never needed to recover.

That gap between "accurate on the data" and "competent in the loop" is the whole of [Module 9](../09-robot-learning/01-behavior-cloning.md), met here on the first day.

The detector is *exact*. Zero error, every frame. The robot still hits things at 2 m/s and above, and stops safely at 1 m/s — which reads as a perception problem that gets worse at speed, and sends the team shopping for a better camera. It is arithmetic: 0.30 s of pipeline latency at 2 m/s is 0.60 m of ground already covered, so a perfect measurement of where the obstacle *was* is a 0.60 m error about where it *is*. Latency you can measure is latency you can compensate.

<code-exercise src="tr-l5-mean-hides-it"></code-exercise>

The selection script has worked for a year. It scores candidates on `mean_error + 0.01 * mean_violations`, which makes an envelope violation worth 0.0018 — smaller than the noise between candidates. Averaging a hard constraint over forty episodes is how a physical limit becomes a rounding error, and the fix is not a bigger weight. There is no weight that works, because any weight can be bought back by enough accuracy. Filter, then rank.

## E. Debrief: the method

**Ask where the data came from before asking what the model did with it.** Bug 1 is invisible until you notice that the evaluation states were generated by the expert. This single question — *whose actions produced these states?* — separates offline metrics from closed-loop ones, and it is the question behind everything in [Module 10](../10-evaluation/01-statistical-rigor.md).

**When a failure scales with speed, suspect time, not accuracy.** Bug 2's signature is that slowing down fixes it. Anything that improves when you go slower is about how long something takes, not how correct it is — and slowing the robot down is a real fix that nobody wants to ship.

**Constraints and objectives are different kinds of thing.** Bug 3 puts a safety limit inside a weighted sum, where it can always be traded away. This is not a tuning error, and the correct response is structural: filter the unsafe candidates out, then optimise over what remains. If nothing remains, say so — *"none of these are shippable"* is a valid and often correct output, and [11.5](../11-deployment/05-safety-cases.md) builds the whole safety-case argument on that distinction.

## F. Graded work & portfolio extension

**Graded:** the three fixes are Module 0's diagnostic assessment. None of them require robotics knowledge you don't already have — which is the point. They require noticing which assumption stopped holding.

**Portfolio:** take bug 1 further and plot the divergence curve — worst |y| against rollout length, for several per-step error magnitudes. The family of curves shows the O(εT²) shape that [lesson 9.1](../09-robot-learning/01-behavior-cloning.md) derives, measured rather than asserted, and it is a good first figure for a portfolio because it explains a real phenomenon with about twenty lines of code.
