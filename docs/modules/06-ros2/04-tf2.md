## A. Why this matters

[Lesson 1.3](../01-geometry/03-transform-trees.md) built a transform tree: frames as nodes, transforms as edges, a lookup as a walk between them. tf2 is that tree with one thing added, and the addition is the entire lesson.

**Transforms have timestamps.** The tree is not a structure, it is a *history* — a rolling buffer of where every frame was, with a lookup that takes a time argument. Get that argument wrong and everything still works: no exception, no warning, a perfectly plausible answer that is most of a metre from the truth.

## B. The lookup has three answers

`lookupTransform(target, source, time)` can return a transform, or fail in two quite different ways:

| | what it means | what to do |
|---|---|---|
| **ok** | the time is inside the buffer | use it |
| **future** | newer than anything received | **wait** — it is coming |
| **past** | older than the cache keeps | give up; it is gone |

The distinction matters because the two failures need opposite responses. A "future" lookup is a normal startup condition and a race you win by waiting a few milliseconds (`canTransform` with a timeout). A "past" lookup is unrecoverable — tf2 keeps ten seconds by default, and a detector that fell fifteen seconds behind, or a bag replayed slowly, is asking for a transform nobody has any more.

Catching both with one `except` and logging "tf error" throws away the only information that tells you which one you have.

## C. `Time(0)` is not "now"

`Time(0)` means *the latest transform available*, and it is the default people reach for because it never throws. Here is what it costs.

A robot driving 1.5 m/s with 0.8 rad/s of yaw. A scan is captured, a detector takes 80 ms, and the result is transformed with the latest transform instead of the one at the capture timestamp:

```
scan captured at 8.000 s, transformed at 8.080 s

  a detection   0.0 m away lands  0.120 m from where it is
  a detection   1.0 m away lands  0.136 m from where it is
  a detection   5.0 m away lands  0.342 m from where it is
  a detection  10.0 m away lands  0.651 m from where it is
  a detection  20.0 m away lands  1.285 m from where it is
```

At the sensor the error is just how far the robot drove: 12 cm. At 10 m it is **65 cm**, five times worse, and the extra is entirely rotation — 0.064 rad of yaw over 80 ms is 64 cm of arc at that range. **This failure is much worse for a lidar than for a bumper**, and it gets worse the further away the interesting things are.

The fix is one argument. Look the transform up at the message's timestamp and the error is exactly zero.

### The price of "latest", in centimetres

The time argument's whole purpose can be stated as a table. This exercise's
robot drives a constant arc at 1.5 m/s and 0.8 rad/s; a scan is captured, a
detector chews on it, and then someone transforms the detections. Looking up
the transform at "now" instead of at capture time costs:

| Processing delay | Base has moved | Yaw has turned | A point at 5 m is misplaced by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20 ms | 3 cm | 0.9° | **8+ cm** |
| 80 ms (this exercise's detector) | 12 cm | 3.7° | **32+ cm** |
| 200 ms (a heavy neural detector) | 30 cm | 9.2° | **80+ cm** |

The rotation is the killer, not the translation: at 5 m of range the yaw
error's lever arm dominates, which is why the damage grows with detection
*distance*, not just with delay. Thirty-two centimetres is the width of an
obstacle; eighty is a doorway. And every centimetre of it vanishes with the
same one-line change — look up the transform *at the scan's timestamp* and
let the buffer interpolate — because the robot's pose at capture time is
sitting in the buffer either way. `lookup_transform('map', 'lidar',
scan.header.stamp)` versus `Time()` is the cheapest 32 cm you will ever buy,
and the smeared-walls signature from lesson 1.1 is what it looks like when
someone doesn't.

## D. How much latency can you actually afford?

Run it backwards: for a 10 cm error budget, how long may the detector take?

| range | affordable latency |
|---:|---:|
| 1 m | 58 ms |
| 10 m | **12 ms** |

At 10 m you have less than one control period. So `Time(0)` on this robot is not a shortcut with a small cost — it is unusable at range, and no amount of making the detector faster fixes a pipeline that discards the timestamp it was given.

This is also a useful way to read a perception latency budget. [11.1](../11-deployment/01-latency-budgets.md) asks whether the loop closes in time; this asks what the loop's latency does to the *geometry*, and the answer depends on how fast you are turning and how far away you are looking.

## F. In production

- **Always look up at the message's timestamp.** `Time(0)` belongs in visualization and almost nowhere else.
- **Use `canTransform` with a timeout** for the "future" case, and handle "past" separately — they are different bugs.
- **Static transforms go on `/tf_static`**, published once with `transient_local` durability. A sensor's mounting does not need republishing at 50 Hz, and a node that starts late still needs it — which is [6.2](02-qos.md)'s durability argument, in the place you meet it first.
- **One publisher per edge.** Two nodes publishing `odom → base_link` produce a tree that flickers between two answers, and the symptom is a robot that jitters for no reason in the logs.
- **Lengthen the buffer if you replay bags slowly**, or you will hit "past" on data you definitely still have.
- **`tf2_tools view_frames` and `tf2_echo`** answer "who publishes this edge and how old is it" faster than reading code.

## G. Experiment

Take your own perception pipeline's measured latency and your robot's typical yaw rate, and compute the affordable-latency table for the ranges you care about. Then check what your code actually passes as the time argument. The interesting outcome is not usually that the code is wrong — it is discovering that the budget is 12 ms when you had assumed the timestamp was a formality.

## H. Failure modes

- **`Time(0)` on sensor data.** Silent, plausible, and proportional to range and yaw rate.
- **Catching both failures as one.** "Future" wants a wait and "past" wants a redesign.
- **Static transforms published `volatile`.** Fine until a node starts late.
- **Two publishers on one edge.** A tree with two answers.
- **A buffer shorter than your worst-case processing lag.** The transform existed; you asked too late.
- **Assuming the tree is a tree.** It is a tree *per instant*; the version you get depends on when you ask.
